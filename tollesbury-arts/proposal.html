The proposal

What we would build

A proper online gallery at tollesbury.arts, where every artist on the Trail has their own page and their own listings, and visitors can buy work with a card in the ordinary way. The walking-tour app at tollesbury.app links across to it, so someone who enjoys the Trail in June can still buy from those artists in November.

The gallery sells on the artists' behalf as their agent. One basket, one checkout, one set of postage rules — but every piece is clearly marked with the artist who made it, and the money is theirs from the moment it arrives.

Taking part

One commission rate, two ways to join

Every artist pays the same 10% on a sale. No tiers, no crossover point, nothing to work out. The only choice is whether to pay a little more up front for a more prominent place in the gallery.

Standard

£20 / year

Commission 10%

A listing for every piece, your own artist page, and your name on every sale.

Featured

£60 / year

Commission 10%

Everything above, plus rotation on the gallery homepage and a fuller profile page with a photograph and artist statement.

This is a deliberate simplification. An earlier draft charged 5% and 15% depending on tier — but charging two different rates requires a paid plugin licence at £118 a year, which was consuming two-thirds of the gallery's entire annual margin. A single rate runs on the free version, saves roughly £230 a year once the extra commission is counted, and lowers the number of artists we need before the gallery pays for itself from 27 to 23.

10% compares very favourably with a commercial gallery, which typically takes 40–50% of the sale price and pays out on its own schedule. In year one we suggest waiving joining fees entirely as a launch incentive — it is the fastest way to reach the numbers the gallery needs.

The money

How a sale reaches the artist

  1. 01

    A buyer pays by card

    Stripe handles the card details. The gallery never sees or stores them. The buyer's purchase is complete at that moment — nothing about their contract depends on when the artist is paid.

  2. 02

    The money sits in the company's account, ring-fenced

    It is the artist's money held in trust, not the gallery's income. It is never used as working capital, and it appears separately in the accounts.

  3. 03

    The artist packs and posts the work

    Postage is charged at the checkout using gallery-wide flat rates by size, so nobody has to guess. Larger pieces can be marked collection-only.

  4. 04

    Artists are paid quarterly, with a statement

    The same rhythm the village gallery already works to, so it will feel familiar. Commission and card fees are shown as separate lines, so there is nothing to query.

Look and feel

Design, and who can actually use it

We would buy a professionally designed theme rather than build one from scratch — it is a fraction of the cost and gets us a far better-looking site. But the deciding factor is not how the demo looks. It is whether a visitor who cannot use a mouse, or who reads the screen at 200%, or who is squinting at a phone on the sea wall, can still buy a picture.

That matters more than usual for us. A good number of the Trail's artists and its visitors are older. A gallery nobody can use is not a gallery.

Searching for a theme that does both turned up an awkward finding. Of the twenty-nine marketplace themes that advertise support for the multi-vendor plugin we need, not one mentions accessibility anywhere. Of the ninety-five themes that do claim accessibility, not one mentions the plugin. There is no theme that satisfies both, so the question is which gap we close ourselves.

Our pick

Start from a theme built to a published accessibility standard, then make the artists' shop pages sit inside it. The reverse — bolting accessibility onto a marketplace theme — means fighting the page builder and the menus it generates, which is days of work with no guarantee of success.

  • Ileana $66 Passes WCAG 2.2 level AA, and built specifically for local shops selling handmade and artisan work — which is precisely us. Last updated a fortnight ago. It is also a modern "block" theme, which matters: WooCommerce is moving steadily to blocks with every release, so this is the version that ages well over the years a volunteer will be maintaining it.
  • Icelander $66 The cautious alternative. Same standard and same author, with 1,249 sales behind it rather than 22. But it is an older-style theme the author is candid about keeping low in his update queue, so it is the safer choice today and the weaker one in three years.

We asked the author directly. He was refreshingly straight: none of his themes have been tested with the multi-vendor plugin we need, and he has no way of knowing whether any customer uses the two together. He suggested we test it ourselves using one of his free themes first — if the free one works with the plugin, the paid one will. That gives us a way to settle the only real unknown for nothing but an afternoon, before spending a penny.

Considered and set aside

On accessibility

Worth recording what was looked at, and why it was not chosen.

  • WoodMart $69 118,000 sales and support for every multi-vendor plugin going — on adoption alone it would win easily. Makes no accessibility claim at all, and its menus and page-builder markup are exactly the parts that are hardest to fix afterwards.
  • Martfury $59 15,000 sales and the best-known marketplace theme. Same objection.
  • Gesso, Ozeum, Musea and similar $69–85 The handsome gallery and museum themes. Beautiful demos, but built to show art rather than sell it from many separate sellers, and none makes an accessibility claim either. Worth browsing for design ideas we can borrow.

A theme licence is a one-off $66, about £52, and is already inside the budget below.

One caution the committee should hear. A theme is only a wrapper. A perfectly accessible theme filled with photographs that have no written description still fails a blind visitor completely. So the artist submission form will require a one-line description of every picture, and that single habit will do more for real accessibility than the theme choice does. Whichever theme we pick, the committee should see it working with our own artists' pictures in it before we sign it off.

On the legal position: the Equality Act 2010 asks service providers to make reasonable adjustments rather than naming a standard, and the regulations that bind councils and public bodies do not catch a company like ours. So nothing forces our hand. But WCAG 2.2 AA is the recognised benchmark for what "reasonable" means, it is the right thing for a village that includes a lot of older people, and it reads very well in a funding application.

Principles

How the gallery will behave

Three commitments I would like the committee to hold me to, and to hold the gallery to after I have handed it over. They are the same idea pointed at three different ways of letting people down.

  1. Everyone can use it

    Built to WCAG 2.2 level AA: usable by keyboard alone, readable by a screen reader, legible at 200% zoom, and tested for colour contrast. Every picture carries a written description, because a photograph with no words is invisible to some of our visitors.

  2. Nobody is pressured

    No countdown timers, no "three people are looking at this", no invented scarcity. When the gallery says a piece is the only one, that is simply true — it is an original. Honest scarcity sells art perfectly well, and the manufactured kind is now a matter for the Competition and Markets Authority rather than a matter of taste.

  3. Buyers know who they are paying and what it costs

    The artist's name on every listing and every confirmation. Postage shown before checkout, never added at the last step. No fee appears late in the process that was not visible at the start.

Technology

Artists can run their own shop

The gallery runs on WordPress and WooCommerce — the same software behind a large share of the world's small online shops, and something I already know well. On top of that sits a plugin called Dokan, which turns a single shop into a marketplace of separate sellers.

What that means in practice: each artist gets their own store page, their own listings and their own orders, and they cannot see or alter anyone else's. If an artist wants to add a new piece, change a price or mark something sold, they can log in and do it themselves. The free version is on the WordPress plugin directory if anyone would like to look, and the free version is what we would use. Dokan's support team have confirmed in writing that it handles a single commission rate applied across all artists, which is exactly what we need — so the plugin costs the gallery nothing at all, and there is no annual licence to remember to renew.

One known gap, now confirmed rather than suspected: Dokan have told us they have no accessibility statement for the artist dashboard, and no competing plugin has one either. They have invited us to file it as a feature request, which we will. That dashboard is a back-office screen used by a handful of people rather than by the public, so it is the right place to accept a compromise — but the committee should know it exists rather than discover it later.

My honest recommendation is that we do not rely on that at the start. Several of our artists have no shop, no social media and do not check email often. If we depend on them logging in to spot an order, work will go unposted and we will hear about it from an unhappy buyer. So for the first year a volunteer lists work on artists' behalf from a simple paper or online form, and orders are phoned or texted through. Dokan have confirmed an administrator can create listings on an artist's behalf with the commission still calculating correctly, so this is a supported way of working rather than a workaround.

There is also a Dokan phone app that sends a push notification when something sells. For artists who are comfortable with a smartphone that may well beat both email and a phone call, and it is free. It is worth offering to anyone who wants it — but it should not be the thing the gallery depends on, because installing an app is exactly the step that will lose the people we are most trying to help. The volunteer and the telephone remain the system; the app is a convenience on top.

The self-service dashboard is there, ready, for the artists who want it — and more will want it once they have seen it working.

Funding

Where the money comes from

The company is not being asked to find £4,691. The plan is two grants, and neither is speculative — both funders are open to applications now, and both have been checked against our circumstances.

Why two grants rather than one. Taylor's Charity is small — it distributed around £5,900 in the whole of last year — so asking it for the full amount would be neither realistic nor fair. A grant of £1,000 to £1,500 is proportionate. It also does something the money alone doesn't: a village award is the strongest possible evidence for the district application. It says Tollesbury has already backed this with its own money, which is far more persuasive to a council officer than any amount of argument from me.

Maldon's fund goes up to £10,000 and explicitly accepts applications from registered social enterprises, which our company will be. It asks that projects show wider community benefit rather than benefit to a particular club — so the application will lead on keeping the village's creative life visible all year and drawing visitors to Tollesbury, which is the honest description of what this is.

The one genuinely urgent decision

The Maldon window closes on 30 September 2026. To meet it, the company needs to be incorporated and I need the committee's approval to apply. Everything else in this proposal can wait a month. This cannot, and the next round would put us a year behind.

If neither application succeeds, nobody is out of pocket. The build contract would be conditional on a grant being awarded, so the company carries no risk and no debt. We would simply try again in the next round, and I would keep the proposal on the shelf until then.

Test it yourself

Move the numbers and see what happens

Every figure in this proposal rests on estimates, and estimates deserve to be challenged. Rather than ask the committee to take mine on trust, here they are to push around. Change the number of artists, the joining fees, the commission rate or how much we think the gallery will sell, and watch the bottom line move.

The one to try first is artists. Income turns out to depend far more on how many people join than on how much gets sold — which tells us where the effort belongs.

Running cost is £1,000 of fixed annual spend — hosting, domains, mailboxes, data protection fee, the maintenance retainer and a contingency — plus card processing at 1.5% and 20p per order, assuming an average sale of £150. Straight talk

Three things I want to be honest about

The margin is thin

At thirty artists and a modest £4,000 of sales, the gallery clears about £330 a year. That is a small reserve, not a profit. Break-even is around twenty-three artists, so there is some room — but not a lot, and the calculator above shows how quickly it disappears if fewer people join than we hope.

Joining fee plus commission is more than the headline rate

An artist selling a little will find that fees and commission together come to around a third of what they sell in the first year, not the 10% on the tin. That is still better than a commercial gallery, and it falls sharply as sales grow — the calculator shows exactly where. We should say so ourselves at the artists' meeting rather than let someone work it out and feel misled.

It needs more than one person

I can build and maintain it. I should not also be the person packing enquiries, chasing artists and answering the phone. If the committee cannot find someone to own the day-to-day running, I would rather we did not start.
