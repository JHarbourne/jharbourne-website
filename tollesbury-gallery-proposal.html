The proposal

What we would build

A proper online gallery at tollesbury.arts, where every artist on the Trail has their own page and their own listings, and visitors can buy work with a card in the ordinary way. The walking-tour app at tollesbury.app links across to it, so someone who enjoys the Trail can still buy from those artists months later.

The gallery sells on the artists' behalf as their agent. One basket, one checkout, one set of postage rules, but every piece is clearly marked with the artist who made it, and the money is theirs from the moment it arrives.

Taking part

Two ways for an artist to join

Artists choose whichever suits how much they expect to sell. The break-even between the two is around £400 of sales a year, so the choice is honest either way.

Pay as you go

£20 / year

Commission 15%

For artists testing the water, or selling occasional lower-priced work.

Member

£60 / year

Commission 5%

For artists who expect to sell regularly, or whose pieces carry higher prices.

Both compare very favourably with a commercial gallery, which typically takes 40 to 50 per cent of the sale price and pays out on its own schedule. In year one we suggest waiving joining fees entirely as a launch incentive: it is the fastest way to reach the numbers the gallery needs.

The money

How a sale reaches the artist

  1. 01

    A buyer pays by card

    Stripe handles the card details. The gallery never sees or stores them. The buyer's purchase is complete at that moment: nothing about their contract depends on when the artist is paid.

  2. 02

    The money sits in the company's account, ring-fenced

    It is the artist's money held in trust, not the gallery's income. It is never used as working capital, and it appears separately in the accounts.

  3. 03

    The artist packs and posts the work

    Postage is charged at the checkout using gallery-wide flat rates by size, so nobody has to guess. Larger pieces can be marked collection-only.

  4. 04

    Artists are paid quarterly, with a statement

    The same rhythm the village gallery already works to, so it will feel familiar. Commission and card fees are shown as separate lines, so there is nothing to query.

Look and feel

The design

We would buy a professionally designed theme rather than build one from scratch. It is a fraction of the cost and gets us a far better-looking site. There is a real choice to make here, and it is worth the committee understanding it, because the prettiest option is not the most practical one.

Recommended: a shop theme, styled as a gallery

Our pick

Built for selling and for multiple sellers from the outset, then styled to look like a gallery. Less exciting to look at on the demo site, far less trouble to live with.

  • WoodMart $69 118,000 sales, rated 4.91, updated last month. Explicitly supports the multi-vendor plugin we need, so artists' own shop pages work out of the box. This is the safe, well-supported choice.
  • Uncode $59 138,000 sales. A designer's theme with an enormous following: the most visually flexible option if we want something more unusual.

The beautiful ones: gallery and museum themes

Extra work

These look wonderful, and they are worth a look so the committee can see what is possible. But they are built to show art rather than sell it from many separate sellers, so making artists' individual shops work inside them adds around two days to the build.

  • Gesso, Art & Print Shop $85 The closest to what we are actually doing: artist portfolios alongside a print shop.
  • Ozeum, Art Gallery & Museum $69 Handsome exhibition layouts and a virtual tour feature that might suit the Trail itself.
  • Arte, Gallery & Museum $69 An Awwwards mention for arts. Uses an older page builder, which counts against it.
  • Musea, Art Gallery & Museum $85 Includes exhibition ticketing, which could be useful for Trail events later on.

A theme licence is a one-off cost of £45 to £70 and is already inside the budget below. Whichever we choose, the committee should see it working with our own artists' pictures in it before we sign it off.

Technology

Artists can run their own shop

The gallery runs on WordPress and WooCommerce, the same software behind a large share of the world's small online shops, and something I already know well. On top of that sits a plugin called Dokan, which turns a single shop into a marketplace of separate sellers.

What that means in practice: each artist gets their own store page, their own listings and their own orders, and they cannot see or alter anyone else's. If an artist wants to add a new piece, change a price or mark something sold, they can log in and do it themselves. The free version is on the WordPress plugin directory if anyone would like to look; we would use the paid tier, at about £118 a year, because that is what allows the two different commission rates.

My honest recommendation is that we do not rely on that at the start. Several of our artists have no shop, no social media and do not check email often. If we depend on them logging in to spot an order, work will go unposted and we will hear about it from an unhappy buyer. So for the first year a volunteer lists work on artists' behalf from a simple paper or online form, and orders are phoned or texted through. The self-service dashboard is there, ready, for the artists who want it, and more will want it once they have seen it working.

Next steps

What I need from the committee

  • Agreement in principleThat the Trail wants an online gallery on these terms, and that the company will own and run it.
  • A named volunteer for ordersSomeone other than me to handle orders and ring artists. This is the single condition I would attach to the build: without it, the gallery will fail on operations rather than technology.
  • Approval to apply for fundingThe Taylor Fund first, then other village and county sources. I will draft the applications. The build contract would be conditional on a grant being awarded, so the company carries no risk.
  • A decision on the artist tiersThe £20 / 15% and £60 / 5% structure, with fees waived in year one.
  • Sign-off on the artist agreementA short contract each artist signs. It is the document that keeps the tax, financial and consumer-law positions clean, and it needs the committee behind it.

Straight talk

Three things I want to be honest about

The margin is thin

At thirty artists and a modest £4,000 of sales, the gallery clears about £181 a year. It funds itself and no more. Break-even is around twenty-seven artists, uncomfortably close to what we expect to have. Raising the pay-as-you-go joining fee to £30 would give us more room, and I would suggest we consider it.

Joining fee plus commission is more than the headline rate

An artist selling a little will find that fees and commission together come to around a third of what they sell in the first year. That is still better than a commercial gallery, and it falls sharply as sales grow, but we should say so ourselves at the artists' meeting rather than let someone work it out and feel misled.

It needs more than one person

I can build and maintain it. I should not also be the person packing enquiries, chasing artists and answering the phone. If the committee cannot find someone to own the day-to-day running, I would rather we did not start.
